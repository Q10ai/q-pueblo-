Questions to take

  • Which routes, aircraft, seats and departure times are proposed?
  • What are total fares, bag costs, through-ticketing and missed connection protections?
  • What completion and on-time rates has each carrier achieved on comparable routes?
  • How will wheelchair users and families be accommodated?
  • What is the requested annual subsidy, and what will sustain EAS eligibility?
  • Where can the public read all bids and submit written comments, and by what deadline?

Why the decision matters

The federal EAS program supports air access for eligible communities. Recent changes constrain certain waivers beginning October 1, 2026; confirm Pueblo’s formal eligibility, waiver record and actual subsidy per passenger with DOT. [S3]

CDOT estimates airport-wide economic impacts of 838 jobs and $226.095 million in business revenue. These are broader modeled aviation impacts, not the value of one airline. PCC separately reports $471,423 in approved state support for aviation maintenance training aiming for 45 jobs over three years. Actual jobs and cohort start remain to be checked. [S5–S8]

40 research perspectives

1. What this is about

Pueblo's next Essential Air Service (EAS) provider selection, a public engagement event and the future of scheduled air service; not a closure notice. [S1,S2]

2. The main finding

Denver Air Connection's existing order runs to March 31, 2027. The public can give input at an announced December 15 open house. Neither a new winner nor the 2027 subsidy is known. [S1,S2]

3. How it developed

The preceding Southern Airways contract specified 24 weekly round trips with nine-seat aircraft. In 2024 five carriers submitted proposals; DOT chose DAC in March 2025. Those are historical bidders, not a 2026 shortlist. [S2]

4. What is happening now

The supplied Fly Pueblo notice invites residents to meet bidding airlines. The airport still lists DAC; verify the proposed schedules and attendance with airport staff. [S1,S4]

5. Important dates

March 17, 2025 selection order; April 1, 2025 contract start; December 15, 2026 open house 11 am–1 pm; March 31, 2027 scheduled contract end. New order and comment cutoff not established. [S1,S2]

6. Who is affected

Potential passengers, families, employers, students, airport workers, visitors and residents of the surrounding region. Quantify actual users and unmet demand rather than assuming all residents benefit.

7. Children and future generations

Assess travel for specialist appointments, educational trips, family reunification, and access to aviation career pathways. Track affordability and cancellations without collecting children's personal details; no measured child outcome yet.

8. Families and workers

Compare door-to-door costs, work time lost, missed connections, accessible transport and baggage costs. A household without a car may face different choices from one driving to Denver.

9. Neighborhoods and wider region

Pueblo Memorial is east of the city. Include Pueblo West and southeastern Colorado in a voluntary origin survey; do not assign the whole region's demand to the airport. [S11]

10. Fairness and access

Ask each bidder for wheelchair boarding arrangements, accessible lavatories where applicable, assistance procedures, accessible booking and fares; evaluate actual performance, not aircraft size alone.

11. Who is responsible

City Aviation manages local airport operations; carriers deliver service; U.S. DOT administers the EAS award. Airport Advisory Committee provides recommendations. [S3,S9,S11]

12. Who can make decisions

DOT selects the provider; City officials and actual or prospective passengers can submit preferences. The open house itself is not a final vote or award. [S3]

13. Rules and requirements

DOT describes an average of at least 10 enplanements per service day for communities not exempt by distance, and a subsidy-per-passenger threshold below $650 for communities less than 175 miles from the nearest large or medium hub. Confirm Pueblo's official distance and calculation. [S3]

14. Promises and commitments

Current order specifies twelve nonstop weekly round trips to Denver, 30- or 50-seat aircraft; scheduled service is not the same as completed service. [S2]

15. Oversight and accountability

Seek the next selection order, local recommendation, comment summary, contract compliance, and Airport Advisory Committee records. Separate city lease and federal EAS contract. [S2,S3,S9]

16. Where money comes from

The EAS subsidy is federally funded. Municipal operations, leases, capital grants and local matching funds have separate accounting. Do not assign the airline subsidy to the City's general fund. [S2,S3]

17. Costs to public and households

Existing second-year annual subsidy rate is $6,836,498. Household cost should include ticket, parking, ground transport, time, baggage and expected disruption; no current fare quote is implied. [S2]

18. What has been approved

Order 2025-3-7 specifies year-one $6,449,526 and year-two $6,836,498, a nominal two-year total of $13,286,024. Future bid amounts not verified. [S2]

19. What has actually been paid

DOT generally compensates compliant completed segments after service. Obtain actual claims, adjustments, payments and fiscal-year denominator; the announced annual subsidy is not proof of cash disbursement. [S3]

20. Benefits and value

CDOT's 2025 study estimates 838 jobs and $226.095m business revenue associated with the overall airport. These figures include wider aviation activity and are not a measure of EAS-only benefit or loss. [S5,S6]

21. How the service works

Air carriers submit proposals for routes, schedule, equipment and requested support; DOT solicits preferences and evaluates the statutory factors before selecting. [S3]

22. Capacity and availability

Current contracted frequency implies 624 scheduled departures per 52-week illustrative year, each 30 or 50 seats, or 18,720–31,200 outbound seats before actual schedule deviations. Do not confuse available seats with people flown. [S2]

23. Quality and actual results

Publish monthly completion and on-time rates, actual boardings, accessible-service metrics and missed connection reports. A secondary route compilation reports low utilization, but official EAS metrics remain unverified. [S12]

24. Reliability and maintenance

Compare spare aircraft, replacement crew, weather planning, incident procedures and hub recovery as service-quality considerations. Do not suggest operations in unsafe conditions.

25. Delays and obstacles

Key unknowns are the formal solicitation, bidder identities, proposed schedules, airport performance, applicable waivers and official award timing. [S10]

26. Connections to other issues

Rail, buses, highway travel, tourism, workforce training, economic development, airport capital planning and regional fiscal resilience are interconnected but distinct funding instruments.

27. Energy and resource needs

Compare measured fuel per passenger trip and airport operating resources across equivalent itineraries, including driving to substitute airports. No energy score without data.

28. Environmental and physical effects

Assess noise, air emissions, airport pavement, water and stormwater obligations through appropriate official studies. No new construction or quantified environmental impact is established by the open house.

29. Risks and unintended effects

Low ridership, rising subsidy, insufficient connection windows, affordability barriers or service gaps may create feedback loops. These are conditional risks, not findings.

30. Long-term consequences

Continued useful connectivity could support workforce and business access; loss of service could increase road travel. A scheduled airline disruption does not imply closure of general aviation, cargo or training.

31. Available alternatives

Compare any actual bids, a continued Denver link, other viable hub proposals, alternate EAS only if available and authorized, and ground connections as contingency. No alternative described here is a confirmed bid.

32. Tradeoffs between options

Balance frequency, larger aircraft, competitive fares, accessible boarding, reliable connections, subsidy and transition risk. Avoid recommending a carrier before seeing comparable bids.

33. Lessons from elsewhere

Use similar EAS communities as benchmarks only after normalizing geography, service days, aircraft and passenger reporting; no peer performance claim is made.

34. What could improve the situation

Create a common bid table, publish monthly performance and eligibility data, survey unused demand, and test last-mile airport transport. Start with existing data resources and reversible pilots.

35. What people can do

Attend the December 15 event; call 719-553-2760 if unable to attend and ask how to submit written comments plus the official deadline. Use the known DOT docket number to find filed material. [S1,S10,S11]

36. How strong the evidence is

Contract and national rules are primary official sources. Event date is supported by supplied announcement only; latest bid documents and eligibility calculations not retrieved. Study economic values are estimates.

37. Conflicting information and other explanations

In 2024 the City favored Breeze but DOT selected DAC after weighing criteria including cost. Public preference matters but is not dispositive. Current performance requires current evidence. [S2]

38. What remains unknown

FY2026 DOT passenger denominator, actual eligible payments, waiver history, 2026 bidders, local operating net cost, response plans, comment procedure and future award.

39. What would change the conclusion

New solicitation or award, formal eligibility/waiver status, audited performance data, city recommendation, updated event notice or documented route economics trigger an amendment.

40. Qified insight

Treat commercial air service as a bounded regional access system: service design influences ridership, ridership affects funding resilience, and funding affects connectivity. Protect separate airport functions and test service options against documented household and public costs.
