REPORT 1 · COUNTY SPENDING AND PUBLIC SERVICES

Can Pueblo County balance its budget and protect public services?

The 2027 plan closes a General Fund gap. Lasting recovery still depends on savings, financing and service results.

Updated November 10, 2026 · Pueblo County government. This is separate from the City of Pueblo budget.

The answer

The County's proposal would balance the General Fund through spending reductions, with a stated $1.265 million annual surplus. That is a plan, not money already saved. Other funds still show negative projected balances, and the starting General Fund balance assumes an asset sale and financing. The available records do not yet establish a durable recovery or unchanged public services.

$1.265MStated annual surplus after reductions

A proposed balanced General Fund is a meaningful step. It is not yet proof of financial recovery across County services.

S1

1. The proposal closes a real starting gap on paper

The detailed budget book lists the amounts in the table above. Its proposed reductions exceed its stated starting shortfall by about $1.265 million. These are proposed budgets for 2027, not a report of savings already delivered.

The book bases its reduction target on eligible personnel costs, including the associated health allocation. Elected offices may meet their dollar targets in different spending categories. A 15% personnel-based target does not mean every department loses 15% of its total budget or that 15% of staff will be dismissed.

S1 · S2

2. The wider financial position remains conditional

The proposal presentation projects the year-end balances shown below. Those are budgetary fund balances, not bank balances or interchangeable pots of unrestricted money. A positive General Fund projection does not clear a negative balance in another fund.

The presentation says its starting General Fund balance uses unaudited 2025 and 2026 projections that include a $7 million asset sale and an anticipated $15 million financing transaction. Their completion and usable net proceeds were not established in this review. They must not be treated as completed cash receipts or reliable recurring revenue.

S2

3. The underlying pressures connect revenue, facilities and services

The County's financial outlook describes declining marijuana-tax receipts, higher insurance and health-related costs, continuing debt payments and growing maintenance demands. Some totals cover several funds; some recent results are unaudited. Those figures do not all measure the General Fund or the same period.

A building or financing decision creates costs after construction. A useful recovery test therefore follows the full chain: construction commitment, financing payment, operating cost, maintenance requirement and service outcome. A lower capital budget can reflect projects ending; it does not automatically measure a reduction in everyday services.

S1 · S2 · S3

4. Small changes could consume the planned cushion

The stated surplus is about 1.03% of proposed General Fund revenue and other sources. Our scenarios below test that margin; they are illustrations, not County forecasts. A 1% revenue shortfall would leave roughly $38,097 of the planned surplus if every other assumption held. If only 80% of the planned reductions were achieved, the illustrative annual result would be about a $697,531 shortfall.

That sensitivity is a reason for frequent actual-to-budget reporting and early service checks. It is not a prediction that the proposal will fail.

S1 · Our calculations

5. The posted documents need a clearer reconciliation

The detailed book uses a $65,409,813 reduction target base; one financial-outlook slide displays $66.96 million. Those are different denominators. We use the book's detailed calculation and ask the County to identify the controlling version and explain the difference.

Whole-dollar arithmetic also differs by $1 from the book's stated shortfall and surplus. Revenue less proposed spending is $1,264,764; the book states $1,264,763. Its rounded presentation figures have additional small differences. We retain the County's stated surplus for our scenarios. The dollar difference does not change this assessment, but the underlying schedule should reconcile before adoption.

What could point in a different direction?

The proposal itself is evidence that the County is planning a response; a projected negative fund balance alone does not establish insolvency or misconduct. Spending reductions may be achieved through contracts, vacancies or process changes rather than a proportional loss of service. The alternatives cannot be resolved without actual spending, implementation and service records.

QIFIED INSIGHT

What the connected picture tells us

Qified means bringing evidence together to explain connections, consequences and useful choices.

What changes when we connect the evidence?

The question is larger than whether one column balances. Test the operating plan, starting resources, payment deadlines, other funds and the services people actually receive together. A strong result in one can hide a problem in another.

Who could feel the effects?

Residents and businesses rely on public safety, road maintenance, permits and family services. Staff, contractors and community partners may carry extra work when a budget changes. These are pathways to investigate, not documented service cuts in this report. Use aggregate waits, workload and service coverage; keep personal case files private.

Where could costs move?

A saving for one office can become overtime, a repair bill, a longer wait or extra work for another provider. For example, postponing maintenance could reduce this year's spending while increasing later repair or service disruption. That is a risk to test, not an observed outcome established here.

Which choices deserve comparison?

Compare an across-the-board dollar target with targeted changes tied to workload, contracts and service obligations. Shared transactions may remove duplicated work; cuts can also create bottlenecks. The County says additional shared-services savings are not included in its proposed result. Do not count them a second time or promise them before a pilot measures cost and quality.

What would a convincing recovery look like?

Recurring operations supported by recurring resources; completed financing identified separately; manageable debt-payment dates; fund-specific reserve progress; and measured service continuity. Proposed policy milestones are not reserves already in place. The book proposes a 10% initial reserve floor and a 16.67% long-term target, with phased rebuilding.

Our recommended next decision

Before treating this as a completed recovery, publish a monthly fund-by-fund bridge from the proposal to actual revenue, spending, usable balances and service results. Start with the General Fund, Road and Bridge, and Human Services. Explain material differences and identify a responsible office and next reporting date.

Show the arithmetic and its limits

Illustrative result = stated surplus − (proposed revenue × revenue shortfall %) − (planned reductions × undelivered share).

Surplus share = $1,264,763 ÷ $122,666,601 = 1.0311%. The proposed spending equals $131,213,309 − $9,811,472 = $121,401,837.

The book’s displayed revenue minus proposed spending is $1,264,764; its stated surplus is $1,264,763. We preserve the stated result and flag the $1 difference. The example is not a cash forecast, reserve estimate or causal prediction.
