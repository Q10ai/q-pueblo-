Electricity affordability, utility accountability and alternative energy pathways in Pueblo

Abstract

This living, non-peer-reviewed public-interest study examines an application to increase base electric revenues in southern Colorado, its distributional consequences, the evidence necessary to evaluate utility costs, and the feasibility of alternative energy arrangements. It uses a regulator summary, applicant notices and statements, local reporting, published feasibility studies and a limited set of comparator sources. The requested increment is $26,663,479 annually; the illustrative 600-kWh bill increases by $10.90 with the proposed refund or $18.30 without it. Neither the requested revenue nor the refund is a final approved tariff. The paper recommends a verifiable rate bridge, cost and maintenance reconciliation, hardship indicators and staged alternatives analysis. No independent prudence audit, field inspection or causal household-outcome study has been completed. [1–3, 10, 23]

1. Research questions and scope

What is changing in the bill? Which costs and investments justify the requested revenue? How are burdens distributed across income, building and customer classes? What alternatives are legally, operationally and economically feasible? The geographic scope is Black Hills Colorado Electric's southern Colorado territory, not all of Pueblo County and not the separate Black Hills Colorado gas utility. The city-only municipalization analysis has a different boundary. Sources checked November 18–19, 2026; recheck by November 25 or earlier on a material docket event.

2. Evidence method

Classify regulator summaries and effective orders separately from applicant proposals, secondary reporting, commissioned studies, calculations and community signals. This update rechecked the filed mailer (both pages), PUC summer newsletter, issuer release, October council reporting, GDS and Brattle executive materials, San Isabel's October account of cost recovery and Xcel's published components. The earlier 22-source registry is retained with its original check dates; unexamined links are not upgraded. Official PUC case hub remains inaccessible; a third-party docket index is useful for locating filings, not for proving their contents. No final order was located. Each calculation uses an explicit denominator, time period and status. Resident comments are qualitative prompts, not a survey, and no names or screenshots enter the public archive.

3. Case chronology and jurisdiction

The applicant filed June 12, 2026 under advice letter 912. The mailer mentions July 13 as the initially proposed tariff date but anticipates new rates in or before March 2027 if approved; the PUC targets spring 2027. The local press reports unanimous October 26 Council opposition to the rate case and separate resource-plan case. City and county intervention motions, trial staff and the Utility Consumer Advocate appear in the docket index; their positions and rulings require primary records. December 1 Cañon City, December 2 Pueblo and January 19 virtual public comment opportunities are reported; verify official details before travel. Public comment is not party intervention or a binding vote. [1,2,4,6,9]

4. Revenue requirement and money stages

A conventional cost-of-service model is RR = prudent operating cost + depreciation + taxes + allowed return x eligible rate base, adjusted for other revenues and regulatory accounting. Requested annual revenue is a change in the revenue requirement, not net profit, gross system revenue, Pueblo-only collections or an appropriation. Separate proposed, authorized, billed, collected, deferred and refunded amounts. Obtain the company revenue bridge and reconcile depreciation, accumulated deferred taxes, working capital, rider roll-ins, forecast test-year changes, allocation, rate-case expense and any disallowances. Never add the $184M reported investment to the $26.7M request as if they were equivalent annual costs. [2,3,23]

5. Capital structure and earnings

The applicant requests a 10.5% return on equity on a capital structure of 51.02% equity and 48.98% debt. The requested equity contribution to an illustrative weighted return is 0.5102 x 0.105 = 5.3571 percentage points before the debt component and other adjustments. For a hypothetical $100M eligible rate base, reducing ROE by one percentage point changes the equity-return component by $510,200 per year, all else equal, before tax gross-up and other interactions. Neither the eligible rate base nor the cost of debt was reconciled; this is a sensitivity, not a claim for savings. Actual earned ROE, regulator-approved ROE and parent-company earnings are different. Request capital structure workpapers, comparable proxy group, allowed rate-base balance, actual earnings and risk adjustments. [23]

6. Bill mechanics and the proposed credit

At 600 kWh the filed bill is $124.18 baseline; $135.08 with GRSA refund; $142.48 without. The fixed charge rises $8.77 to $12, and the two current base tiers (12.159 and 15.807 cents/kWh) become 16 cents/kWh. The change in base components at 600 kWh is $22.628; the change in the filed total without credit is $18.30. The residual difference is not proof of a billing error: riders, roll-ins and adjustments need tariff-by-tariff reconciliation. PUC attributes $29.4M and $8M to two balances ($37.4M total); company rounds or describes approximately $38M and proposes two years of credits. Do not assume the entire system refund is distributed evenly among residential accounts. The notice's small general-service example is 1,502 kWh: $277.47 baseline, $285.66 with credit, $303.99 without. [1–3]

7. Affordability and distribution

An unchanged residential example has $130.80 annual incremental cost with refund or $219.60 without. At gross annual income $20,000 this is 0.654 or 1.098 percentage points of income; at $40,000 it is 0.327 or 0.549 points. Total household energy burden requires electricity plus other household fuels divided by income over the same period. These increments are not claims about residents' observed incomes or poverty. The larger fixed charge affects low-usage customers; tier removal affects usage differently. Renters often lack control over insulation or equipment. Track disconnections, arrears, payment plans, assistance eligibility and uptake, adjustment burdens and medically vulnerable access at privacy-protecting aggregate levels. No causal estimate is available.

8. Reliability, maintenance and prudence

The company reports $184M investment, work on distribution assets and generation and under 95 minutes of average outage duration in 2025. These are applicant-reported outcomes, not an independent audit. Obtain numerator/denominator definitions, major-event exclusions, SAIDI, SAIFI, CAIDI, outage causes, restoration, age and condition of assets and comparative years. Link each capital project from authorization through in-service date, invoices, capitalization, useful life, revenue recovery and outcomes. For vegetation work request approved annual budgets, actual costs, contractor totals, completed versus scheduled miles, general corridors and treatment types, and rate recovery. Exclude critical infrastructure specifics and personal details. A resident seeing no crews near home neither proves absence of system maintenance nor verifies the charge. [3]

9. Matched provider comparison

Xcel publishes R residential fixed and seasonal energy components, but the applicable plan, riders, time-of-use and taxes must be checked. San Isabel reports a $35 grid fee for most residential members and a 1.5-cent/kWh PCA through December 2026, planned to reset after this period; at 600 kWh that adjustment alone is $9 in a month. These demonstrate different recovery structures, not a verified cheapest-provider ranking. The model requires the same billing month, usage profile, meter type, service class, demand and eligibility, tariff effective dates, mandatory riders, taxes, discounts, and service boundaries. Colorado Springs Utilities is a municipal comparator, not an available retail alternative for a Pueblo address. Do not assume a household may switch local distributors. [24–27,29]

10. Ownership alternatives and debt sensitivity

GDS/BBK's city-commissioned April 2025 draft gives $383–503M in 2030 dollars for city-only acquisition-related costs and finds the city-only case infeasible under its assumptions. The utility-sponsored Brattle study estimates over $1B in financing, assumes 2032 commencement and evaluates a distinct financing/valuation framework. Neither is a binding price. Preserve different dates, scopes and exclusions; do not average. As a pure debt-service sensitivity for $400M principal and 30-year amortization, annual level payments are about $23.13M at 4%, $29.06M at 6%, $35.53M at 8%, before power purchases, staff, maintenance, reserves, taxes and transition costs. Analyze asset valuation, condemnation, bond covenants, load loss, interconnection, wholesale power, workforce, licensing and service continuity before any irreversible decision. Regional cooperative or joint-agency options require fresh legal and financial feasibility. [10,28]

11. Resource plans, electrification and growth

Proceeding 26A-0333E is a separate case; the October resolution reporting connects its potential cost to current rate concerns. Distinguish capacity MW from energy MWh and annual revenue from one-time capital. Model coincident peak demand, storage duration and reserve margins, transmission and distribution upgrades, avoided fuel, stranded assets, and customer class allocation. Electrification cannot be assessed solely by higher electric kWh: compare gas savings, heat pump COP by temperature, building envelope, backup heating, equipment and installation cost, useful life, and assistance. For 1,000 kWh of delivered heat, resistance heat needs approximately 1,000 electric kWh and a heat pump with assumed COP 3 needs about 333 kWh, before auxiliary and climate effects. No local appliance prohibition or universal savings is established. Large industrial and AI loads require contract and tariff protections against shifting infrastructure and stranded costs to existing customers. [4,17,22]

12. Cross-sector shock model

Use incremental cash cost rather than adding unrelated percentages. A hypothetical 100 households at the filed example face $13,080 annual additional cost during the credit or $21,960 without it; these are scenario counts, not local population estimates. An organization with 20 identical 1,502-kWh commercial accounts would see $1,965.60 or $6,364.80 incremental annual cost. Link aggregated facility bills to city, county, schools, hospitals, small businesses, cultural institutions and nonprofits; avoid counting the same dollar in a household and a service provider as separate direct expense. Track offsetting grants only when awarded/received. Scenario A no new increase; B requested credit; C full requested base; D additional unrelated stress with individually identified inputs. No forecast of business failure or public service cuts is warranted.

13. Actionable evidence and oversight

Prioritize docket public records via PUC and UCA, then city-held bills/contracts through applicable records process. Ask for rate bridge, testimony, approved tariff sheets and rate-class cost of service; asset and O&M reconciliation; vegetation work and reliability outcome summary; refund eligibility and residual balances; municipal facility cost exposure; historical series with exact tariff versions. Request segregable public material and explanations for withholding; avoid demanding private utility records via an inapplicable public-record law. Ask applicant and intervenors for responses and record conflicting explanations without implying guilt. Use an editable data template with missing fields left blank, not zero.

14. Public participation and consumer protection

Reference case 26AL-0232E at puc.colorado.gov/comments; supplied hearing notice also gives dora_puc_comments@state.co.us and 303-869-3490. Describe personal bill effects with account numbers and sensitive information removed. Ask for written explanation of fixed charge, refund allocation/expiration, ROE and vegetation costs. Hearings collect public input; they do not settle rates. Written comments are encouraged by early January; check official instructions. If payment is difficult, contact provider promptly and check County LEAP and Energy Outreach Colorado eligibility. Budget billing spreads costs, not necessarily reduces them. [2,6–8,18–21]

15. Implementation, governance and decision gates

Q Energy Watch holds dated public docket snapshots; Q Utility Ledger binds source IDs to costs and outcomes; Q Rate Compare only renders complete, effective tariff calculations; Q Bill Lab is an implemented conditional calculator; Q Grid Watch is a proposed aggregate maintenance and reliability registry; Q Energy Relief lists verifiable resources; Q Public Power evaluates legal/financial options; Q Regional Shock publishes scenarios only. EE prioritizes low-cost controlling records. T2Y converts evidence into usable decisions. RX tracks corrections and independent review. No agent, twin import or automatic rate recommendation is activated. Release requires human editorial review, reproducible math, data privacy checks, appropriate links, rollback and clear next review date. Procurement, ownership conversion and system control are outside this publication.

16. Conclusions and open work

The evidence supports a material proposed price increase, a limited-time offset, and a requested return-on-equity parameter. It does not determine whether all costs are prudent, whether the requested values will be approved, whether other providers are cheaper on matched full tariffs, or the actual location and cost of local maintenance. Publish the current dated evidence separately from the research gaps; update after testimony, settlement, tariff and orders. The case is not closed and the packet is not final. Review November 25, before each hearing, and upon any material event.
