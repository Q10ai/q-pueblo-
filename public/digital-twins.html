Digital Twins

Learn how digital representations of assets and places can help us understand systems, compare changes and prepare for the future.

What is a digital twin?

A digital twin represents a real asset, process or system and is connected to changing information about it. A map, static 3D picture, simulation or personal profile may be useful without being a digital twin. AI is optional.

1. Real system

A building, road, utility or industrial process.

2. Data

Maps, observations and time-stamped measurements.

3. Model

A simplified description of relevant properties and connections.

4. Analysis

Compare conditions, evaluate options or simulate scenarios.

5. Human review

Check limitations and decide whether to act.

6. Feedback

Update and correct the model when new evidence arrives.

Where are twins used?

Factories can model machinery; utilities can model capacity; cities can combine physical and geographic information. Helsinki publishes 3D models and open urban data as part of its digital twin work. Explore Helsinki 3D.

Household statistical models and commercial personal profiles are different technologies. An inferred profile is not proof of an individual's circumstances. A public address can relate to public geography, but should not be used to construct a private resident dossier.

How could Pueblo use these methods?

Compare a hypothetical development against housing, transport, water, power, costs and public services. Use dated records, verified capacity, clearly stated assumptions and alternatives. A simulated outcome is not a permit, prediction or decision. Explore the existing Twin Pueblo Future Lab.

What to ask about any digital model

What is its purpose? What is updated, and how often? Who owns the data? What is missing or uncertain? How is accuracy tested? Who makes decisions? Can errors be corrected? How much does the model cost to operate? Q does not provide an operational address-level or resident twin.
