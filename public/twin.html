Use models and AI responsibly

Q is not only about gathering information. It is about governing how information, models and AI are selected, used, checked and corrected. A useful tool must respect human authority and make its limitations visible.

For residents and families

Use public data to understand issues and ask better questions. Check dates, sources and scope; do not submit private information unnecessarily.

For schools and learners

Compare competing explanations, practice source checking and protect student information. AI supports learning, not replacement of student judgment.

For businesses

Start with a concrete need. Compare manual and automated options, costs, licensing, errors, and vendor exit. Keep accountable human oversight.

For public institutions

Define lawful purpose, accountable owner, affected people, procurement and data rules, independent tests, public transparency, correction and appeal. Never let an unvalidated model determine rights, eligibility, enforcement or public spending.

Q's 14 questions for evaluating any model
  1. Purpose and incentives: Who built the model, who pays for it, what does it optimize, and what is outside its objective?
  2. Authority: Who can lawfully decide or act? A data provider, model or Q cannot acquire public authority through software.
  3. Data and provenance: Identify the original source, collection date, geographic boundary, units, license, and version.
  4. Coverage and missing voices: Check missing populations, sampling, accessibility, geographic gaps and whether aggregates hide differences.
  5. Truth and uncertainty: Separate observations, official records, estimates, forecasts and simulations; look for contrary evidence and error bounds.
  6. Independence: Trace shared upstream data; three dashboards using one dataset do not provide three confirmations.
  7. Privacy and security: Use only authorized data; minimize collection, prevent reidentification and avoid individual targeting.
  8. Fairness and child impact: Check who benefits, who bears costs, and impacts on children, vulnerable residents and future generations.
  9. Cost and energy: Compare full lifecycle costs, staff time, compute, energy and water against a simpler baseline.
  10. Usefulness: Ask whether results improve a measurable outcome without weakening truth or human agency.
  11. Consequences: Test direct, indirect and cascading effects, delayed impacts and plausible alternatives.
  12. Control and recourse: Provide human review, correction, appeal, override, shutdown and rollback proportional to risk.
  13. Licensing and portability: Check reuse rights, data export, retention, vendor exit and replacement before relying on a tool.
  14. Monitoring and validation: Test against observed outcomes, record errors and changes, review on a schedule and suspend unsafe or stale uses.

These questions are an evaluation method, not completed ratings or certifications. Unknown details remain unknown until documented.

Choose the least risky useful approach

Read: check existing public documents and dated data. Compare: examine sources and alternative explanations. Test: run an explicitly hypothetical scenario with no real-world action. Review: require an accountable human and relevant experts before high-impact use. Monitor: verify outcomes and record corrections.

Do not use Q or linked models to profile individuals, target voters, infer criminality from associations, expose sensitive locations or make automated consequential decisions. Never assume a commercial provider has Pueblo coverage, a license, or a Q integration.

Try comparing different views of one issue

Suppose a new housing development is proposed. County GIS can show the planning geography; HUD can describe housing needs; Census can provide population context; city finance can show public expenditure; USGS can describe measured river conditions. None alone establishes water capacity, affordability, actual occupancy or the project's overall benefit.

Q's approach is to retain each source's purpose, observation date, coverage, method, uncertainty and original records. Q separates observed facts, official decisions, estimates and hypothetical scenarios, checks for shared upstream sources, and considers second-order effects, affordability, children, future generations and resource use. A model is evidence to examine, not an authority to obey.

What a full calibrated digital twin requires
  1. Precise geographical, jurisdictional, temporal, system and consent boundaries.
  2. Authorized, versioned, documented source layers for infrastructure, law, events, finance, people and resources.
  3. Calibrated equations, competing models, historical backtesting, uncertainty and residual error.
  4. Privacy, child safety, cybersecurity, energy/attention budgets, maintenance and independent verification.
  5. Governed outputs that distinguish actual observations, estimates, scenarios and unsupported ideas.
